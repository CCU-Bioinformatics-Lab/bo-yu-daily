PhyClone: Accurate Bayesian reconstruction of cancer
phylogenies from bulk sequencing

Abstract
Motivation: Cancer is driven by somatic mutations that result in the expansion of genomically distinct sub-populations
of cells called clones. Identifying the clonal composition of tumours and understanding the evolutionary relationships
between clones is a crucial task in cancer genomics. Bulk DNA sequencing is commonly used for studying the clonal
composition of tumours, but it is challenging to infer the genetic relationship between different clones due to the mixture
of different cell populations.
Results: In this work, we introduce a new probabilistic model called PhyClone that can infer clonal phylogenies from
bulk sequencing data. We demonstrate the performance of PhyClone on simulated and real-world datasets and show that
it outperforms previous methods in terms of accuracy and scalability.
Availability and implementation: Source code is available on Github at: https://github.com/Roth-Lab/PhyClone
under the GPL v3.0 license.
Contact: aroth@bccrc.ca

Key words: cancer, phylogenetics, Bayesian non-parametrics



Introduction                                                                                                                               1



Inferring the evolutionary history of cancer cell populations is a fundamental problem in cancer biology (Burrell et al., 2013;            2


McGranahan and Swanton, 2017). High throughput genomics methods been transformative in this effort, providing insights into                3


mechanisms of malignant ontogeny, therapeutic resistance and metastasis (Turajlic et al., 2019). While single-cell sequencing              4


methods exist to precisely characterize tumour evolution (Zahn et al., 2017; Laks et al., 2019), their application has remained            5


limited due to cost and technical challenges when applied to patient tumour samples. Thus, the field remains heavily reliant on            6


bulk sequencing and computational deconvolution of tumours to infer clonal population structure (Dentro et al., 2021; Frankell             7


et al., 2023).                                                                                                                             8


   Early tools for quantifying clonal populations in bulk sequencing focused on clustering mutations that occur in the same                9


proportion of cancer cells (cellular prevalence) (Roth et al., 2014; Deveau et al., 2018; Gillis and Roth, 2020). These approaches         10


assume that mutations with the same cellular prevalence across samples, share the same evolutionary history i.e. originate at the          11


same point in evolutionary time and have the same pattern of mutation loss (Gerstung et al., 2020). While effective for identifying        12


clonal populations, clustering approaches ignore the underlying phylogenetic relationship among clones. Subsequent approaches              13


have attempted to reconstruct the clonal phylogeny from bulk sequencing (Deshwar et al., 2015; Malikic et al., 2015; Popic et al.,         14


2015; Wintersinger et al., 2022; Grigoriadis et al., 2023b). Beyond providing an estimate of the phylogenetic tree, a key benefit of       15


these approaches is that they allow for the genotypes and prevalence of clones to be resolved.                                             16


   Methods for inferring clonal phylogenies from bulk sequencing generally assume that mutations with higher cellular prevalence           17


occur earlier (closer to the root of the phylogeny) than mutations with lower cellular prevalence (McGranahan and Swanton, 2017). A        18


corollary of this assumption is that if the cellular prevalence of two populations exceeds one, the lower cellular prevalence population   19

must be a descendant of the higher cellular prevalence population (Dentro et al., 2017; Tarabichi et al., 2021). This additivity           20


assumption is relatively weak, and cannot for example resolve whether two clonal populations are siblings or ancestor/descendants          21


if the sum of cellular prevalence does not exceed one. This deficiency can partially be addressed by conducting multi-region               22


sequencing (Tarabichi et al., 2021; Cortés-Ciriano et al., 2022), which has now become common practice in bulk cancer evolution           23


studies (Frankell et al., 2023). The key insight is that if the ordering of cellular prevalence values between two populations             24


switches in different samples, the clonal populations must be siblings (Tarabichi et al., 2021). In addition to only providing weak        25


identifiability, the additivity assumption is violated if mutation loss occurs. In the context of cancer evolution, mutation loss can      26


occur due to genomic instability leading to the deletion of large regions of the genome (McGranahan and Swanton, 2017; Tarabichi           27


et al., 2021). Frequent mutation loss has been identified in genomically unstable cancers in several studies (McPherson et al., 2016;      28


Jamal-Hanjani et al., 2017; Gerstung et al., 2020; Watkins et al., 2020).                                                                  29


   In this work we develop a new method for inferring clonal phylogenies based on bulk sequencing. Given that bulk sequencing              30


may only contain information to partially resolve the clonal phylogenetic structure, we adopt a Bayesian statistical approach              31


which allows us to infer a posterior distribution over the set of potential phylogenies compatible with the data. To address the           32


fact the number of clonal populations is unknown, we have developed a non-parametric Bayesian model of clusters related by                 33


a tree structure. A key benefit of our approach is the ability to marginalise the parameters associated with tree nodes (clonal            34


prevalence), allowing us to perform posterior inference in the lower dimensional space of tree topologies. Inference in the collapsed      35


space of discrete clustering trees still requires exploration of an exponential number of tree topologies with variable numbers of         36


nodes. To address this issue, we developed an inference algorithm using particle Gibbs sampling (Andrieu et al., 2010) coupled             37


with an auxiliary variable construction. In addition, our approach allows for partial information about clustering generated by            38


fast non-phylogenetic methods to be incorporated. We demonstrate in the results, that these advances allow our approach to scale           39


favourably in terms of running time and accuracy with increasing numbers of samples. In addition, we show how our proposed                 40


method can be adapted to allow for the potential of mutation loss for accurate inference in real datasets.                                 41




Methods                                                                                                                                    42



The PhyClone model takes as input: i) allelic count data from somatic single nucleotide variants (SNVs); ii) copy number variant           43


(CNV) information; iii) tumour content estimates from bulk sequence data of one or more related tumour samples (Figure 1). Using           44


this information PhyClone infers the posterior distribution of phylogenetic trees relating the clones in the sample. As part of this       45


inference the model infers the number of clones, sets of mutations which originate at each node in the tree, and the phylogenetic          46


ordering of mutations. We do not explicitly infer the prevalence of clones in each sample, as this variable is marginalised during         47


inference. If needed, it can be reinstantiated via maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation or sampling after tree inference.                 48


   We first describe the non-parametric Bayesian tree structured clustering prior distribution used by PhyClone. We then provide           49


a full description of the basic PhyClone model, and several extensions. Finally, we describe the inference strategy. More detailed         50


discussion of the model and inference strategy are provided in the Supplementary Methods.                                                  51




Prior distribution over clone phylogenies                                                                                                  52


PhyClone employs the forest structured Chinese restaurant process (FS-CRP) as a prior over tree topologies and clusters. In this           53


section we briefly describe the generative process that defines the FS-CRP. In the first step of the FS-CRP we partition SNVs into         54


clusters assumed to have the same evolutionary history. To do so SNVs are split into clusters via the Chinese restaurant process           55

(CRP) (Aldous, 1985, pg. 92). In the second step of the FS-CRP, we sample a topology for the clonal phylogeny. To do so a                               56


multifurcating tree with as many nodes as clusters is selected uniformly at random and each cluster is associated to a node. The                        57


sampled tree may exhibit multiple roots, so we use the observation that any rooted forest (that is, any collection of rooted subtrees)                  58


over K nodes can be turned into a single rooted tree over K + 1 nodes by setting all root nodes to be children of a new empty root                      59


node. In the third step we assign clonal prevalence to each node. The clonal prevalence is defined to be the proportion of malignant                    60


cells which originate at a node, or equivalently the proportion of cells with the genotype defined by the union set of mutations                        61


assigned to nodes on the path to the root. To do so a vector of length equal to the number of nodes is sampled from a Dirichlet                         62


distribution. This enforces the constraint that the sum of all clonal prevalence adds to one. To obtain the cellular prevalence, that                   63


is the proportion of malignant cells that harbour an SNV, we move recursively up the tree from the leafs, summing the clonal                            64


prevalence of the node with the cellular prevalence of its children. This corresponds to assumption SNVs are propagated to all                          65


descendants from their clone of origin. The output of this process is a tree structured clustering of SNVs which we refer to as a                       66


clone phylogeny. In the clone phylogeny each node has a collection of originating SNVs, clonal prevalence, and cellular prevalence                      67


defined.                                                                                                                                                68

Pre-clustering                                                                                                                                 92


For computational efficiency, PhyClone is equipped to leverage clustering information from fast non-phylogenetic approaches such               93


as PyClone-VI (Gillis and Roth, 2020).                                                                                                         94


   Our approach enforces that any mutations clustered together by the pre-clustering step will be kept together. However, mutations            95


assigned to different clusters by the pre-clustering algorithm can also be placed together by PhyClone. This allows PhyClone to                96


refine the clustering based on the constraints imposed by the phylogeny. Though we did not make use of this in the experiments,                97


it is theoretically beneficial for PhyClone analyses to run pre-clustering to overcluster the data i.e. generate more clusters than            98


truth. PhyClone can then merge the resulting clusters to obtain the correct clustering.                                                        99

Inference                                                                                                                                      111


To simultaneously infer the posterior distribution of tree topologies and cluster assignments (T and b), we use a sequential Monte             112


Carlo (SMC) algorithm. The SMC algorithm works by evolving a set of particles which are iteratively extended and reweighed,                    113


such that the final iteration yields an approximation of the posterior.                                                                        114


     We assume that a permutation, σ, of the mutation indices [N ] has been given. At algorithmic time t of the SMC procedure we               115


add data point Xσ(t) = xt .                                                                                                                    116


     A key parameter to define for SMC algorithms is the proposal function, q(xt+1 |xt ), which extends the state xt to a new state            117


xt+1 . In the case of our algorithm, this corresponds to choosing which node in the tree to allocate mutation σ(t + 1) to. To propose          118


a new state at time t + 1, we consider all possible states which can be reached by:                                                            119



1.    Adding a data point to an existing root node.                                                                                            120


2.    Creating a new node choosing a possibly empty subset of children from the existing root nodes as children.                               121



     We randomly choose whether to join an existing root node or start a new node. When adding a mutation to an existing root                  122


node, we compute the probabilities of the resulting trees with the mutation attached at each root node. When adding a mutation                 123


to a new node, we randomly sample the set of children. This approach provides a compromise between the quality of trees proposed               124


and the computational complexity.                                                                                                              125




Overcoming the impact of data ordering                                                                                                         126


A limitation of the procedure so far described is that not all trees can be sampled due to the fixed order of the data points.                 127


Specifically, if x = σi and y = σj where i < j then any tree where x is an ancestor of y cannot be sampled given a fixed σ                     128


(illustrated in Supplementary Figures 2–4). For example, the first element of σ can never be a root node unless the sampled tree               129


has only a single node. To address this issue we use a particle Gibbs (PG) sampler to embed our SMC procedure in a more general                130


Markov chain Monte Carlo sampler (Andrieu et al., 2010).                                                                                       131


     At a high level, we treat σ as an auxiliary variable and alternate between sampling σ and (T, b). Sampling of σ is constrained            132


such that the SMC procedure could generate the current configuration of (T, b).                                                                133


     To see that this algorithm yields a sampler that can sample all trees, note that the single node tree can be reached at any               134


iteration of the algorithm. From this configuration, the sampling σ is uniform, thus allowing any ordering of mutations to be chosen           135


for the subsequent update of (T, b).                                                                                                           136




Additional MCMC moves                                                                                                                          137


To improve the mixing of the sampler, it is beneficial to include additional moves in addition to the particle Gibbs updates. We               138


use two moves, neither of which change the number of nodes in the tree.                                                                        139

     The first move is a subtree prune-regraph. To perform this move, we select a node at random from the tree and remove the                   140


subtree rooted at this node. We then compute the probability of the tree which would result from attaching the subtree to a node                141


in the tree. We do this for all nodes in the tree and Gibbs sample a new attachment node.                                                       142


     The second move we consider is to sample new node assignments for each data point in the tree without changing the topology.               143


To do this, we first shuffle the data point ordering. Then, for each data point, if removing it from its current node does not empty            144


the node, we compute the probability of each tree resulting from having added the data point to every node other than its currently             145


assigned one. We use these probabilities to construct a probability distribution from which we then Gibbs sample the new tree                   146


from.                                                                                                                                           147




Experiments                                                                                                                                     148


We    tested   PhyClone   against   three   widely   used   state-of-the-art   methods:     CONIPHER       (Grigoriadis   et   al.,   2023b),   149


Pairtree (Wintersinger et al., 2022), and PhyloWGS (Deshwar et al., 2015). Here we describe the datasets, program settings                      150


and performance metrics used for these benchmarking experiments.                                                                                151




Datasets                                                                                                                                        152


A combination of newly simulated synthetic datasets, previously published synthetic datasets, and real human cancer data were                   153


used to perform benchmarking of the methods.                                                                                                    154



Tree Structured Stick Breaking Data                                                                                                             155


Tree topologies and clusterings were simulated using the tree structured stick breaking (TSSB) prior. The TSSB prior is an                      156


alternative non-parametric Bayesian prior over clusters and tree topologies (Ghahramani et al., 2010). Allelic count data was                   157


simulated as diploid heterozygous mutations with a tumour content of 1.0. Two TSSB datasets were produced. Both datasets                        158


were simulated had a read depth of 1000, sample number levels of [2, 4, 8, 16], and tumour content of 1.0. The small dataset                    159


was simulated with 100 SNVs and the larger had 10,000 SNVs. Additionally, the small TSSB dataset also included runs with 128                    160


samples.                                                                                                                                        161



Pairtree/Pairsim Data                                                                                                                           162


We used previously published data from Wintersinger et al. (2022). The data was split into two groupings, low and high complexity,              163


to reflect the difference in number of mutations, samples, and true tumour clusters/clones involved in each set of experiments. High            164


complexity trials all varied according to: number of clusters, K={3, 10, 30, 100}; number of samples, S={10, 30, 100}; total depth,             165


T={50, 200, 1000}; and number of mutations, M={30, 60, 100, 200, 300, 600, 1000, 2000, 3000, 10000} with four replicates each                   166


for a total of 432 trials. While the low complexity trials varied according to: number of clusters, K={3, 10}; number of samples,               167


S={1, 3, 10}; total depth, T={50, 200, 1000}; and number of mutations, M={30, 60, 100, 200, 300, 1000} - with four replicates                   168


each for a total of 216 trials.                                                                                                                 169



CONIPHER Data                                                                                                                                   170


We used previously published data from the Grigoriadis et al. (2023b,a). We used datasets 1 and 2, each with a total of 150 trials,             171


which we refer to as noise and no-noise datasets respectively. For analysis, we split each dataset into three categories according to           172


number of samples: 2–3, 4–7, and 8+.                                                                                                            173

FS-CRP loss data                                                                                                                              174


Tree topologies and clusterings were simulated using the FS-CRP prior. Read count and copy-number data were simulated using                   175


the same method as the TSSB datasets. Mutational loss was simulated by first randomly selecting a set of mutations equal to the               176


proportion to be lost, a node is then selected from which this set of mutations is to be ‘lost’ from; the loss-node is selected such          177


that it is a descendant node from the node that the lost mutations originate from. All mutations that are members of the lost                 178


set are given positions on the same chromosome, to simulate the genomic locality of subclonal chromosomal region loss. All nodes              179


downstream of (and including) the loss-node then have the cellular prevalence of the lost set of mutations subtracted from the                180


node’s total, to reflect the impact of the having lost the mutations.                                                                         181


    We simulated 100 trials for each level of mutation proportion to lose, with the mutation proportions being [0.0, 0.1, 0.2]. Each          182


trial simulated 600 SNVs, with a depth of 1000, 8 samples, and a tumour content of 1.0.                                                       183



HGSOC Data                                                                                                                                    184


Data used were from a previous single-cell study of High Grade Serous Ovarian Cancer (HGSOC) where ground truth trees were                    185


inferred from single-cell, whole genome, and targeted deep sequencing data (McPherson et al., 2016). Two sets of data were                    186


reconstructed for the HGSOC experiments; one dataset consisted of purely whole genome sequencing (WGS) bulk data, while                       187


the second dataset replaced the WGS allele counts with the targeted deep sequencing counts for the SNVs which were present in                 188


both sets. Pre-processing involved reconstruction of copy-number, read count, and cluster assignment data from the supplementary              189


tables provided with the original study. Method inferred trees were constructed with the total set of processed SNVs per patient,             190


and were then subset down to include only those SNVs that were used to define the ground truth trees from the original publication            191


when computing metrics.                                                                                                                       192




Program Parameters                                                                                                                            193


Each method was allowed 48-hours wall-clock time to complete each individual trial in each dataset. Pre-clustering was performed              194


for synthetic data using PyClone-VI initialised with 100 clusters, and 100 random restarts. For the HGSOC data, we used the                   195


clusters provided in the original publication. Pre-clustered results were supplied to CONIPHER, Pairtree and PhyClone for all                 196


experiments.                                                                                                                                  197


    PhyClone (version 0.5.0) was run with the following parameters: 4 independent chains, 100 burn-in iterations, 5000 MCMC                   198


iterations, Beta-Binomial allele-count density, semi-adapted proposal kernel, final output tree selected by MAP joint-likelihood;             199


and an outlier prior probability of 0.0. For experiments where we consider outliers in the PhyClone model, denoted PhyClone-O,                200


we set the ‘--assign-loss-prob’ flag and accompanying outlier prior probabilities of 0.05 and 0.5 for the low and high loss prior             201


probabilities respectively.                                                                                                                   202


    PhyloWGS, Pairtree and CONIPHER methods were run with default parameters, with the exception of CONIPHER in the                           203


HGSOC experiments, where the ‘min cluster size’ option was set to 3.                                                                          204




Metrics                                                                                                                                       205


Each experimental method was measured against ground truth in order to assess the accuracy of clonal phylogeny reconstruction                 206


and single nucleotide variant (SNV) assignments. The following metrics were employed:                                                         207



•   V-measure: A metric for measuring clustering accuracy (Rosenberg and Hirschberg, 2007)                                                    208

•   Ancestor-Descendant F-Score (AD F-Score): A reconstruction accuracy metric which measures the F1-score of a method’s                    209


    phylogenetic reconstruction from the perspective of SNV ancestor-descendant relationships (Supplementary Figure 5). Positive            210


    cases result when an SNV is assigned to a node that can form an upward path to the node where the corresponding ancestor                211


    SNV is assigned.                                                                                                                        212



    Significance testing for differences in performance for each benchmarking experiment was performed via Friedman-Nemenyi                 213


testing. If the metric being tested demonstrated a global significance difference (p-value < 0.01) as per the Friedman test, a              214

each other. Subsequently, the mean difference was used to determine the direction of said significance (that is, which performed            216


better out of the pair) (Demšar, 2006).                                                                                                    217

    We first analysed the small TSSB dataset with 100 SNVs. We note, this model is equivalent to the PhyloWGS model and should              220


favour that method. Our goal was to compare the accuracy and efficiency of different modelling and inference approaches when                221


data is generated from a well-defined statistical model. PhyClone and PhyloWGS are fully Bayesian models, whereas Pairtree uses             222


a Gaussian approximation to perform MAP estimation, and CONIPHER optimises a heuristic objective. In addition, PhyClone,                    223


Pairtree and CONIPHER are all capable of utilizing pre-clustering of the data to scale inference.                                           224


    The clustering and tree accuracy results for this experiment are summarised in Figure 2. PhyClone significantly outperformed            225


all other methods in terms of V-measure and AD F-score. There was no significant difference in performance between Pairtree                 226


and PhyloWGS for either V-measure or AD F-score. CONIPHER was significantly outperformed by all methods in terms of both                    227


metrics. PhyloWGS was significantly slower than all other methods, requiring over an order of magnitude more time to finish than            228


other approaches, with the gap widening with larger numbers of samples (Supplementary Figure 6).                                            229


    This experiment supports the idea that fully Bayesian inference can be more accurate. Furthermore, pre-clustering of data               230


can accelerate inference without compromising accuracy. Based on the long-running times observed for PhyloWGS, and the fact                 231


performance was not significantly better than other approaches under conditions which favour it, we did not include it in subsequent        232

  Discussion                                                                                                                                                 279



  In this work we have presented PhyClone, a new statistical method for reconstructing clonal phylogenies from multi-sample bulk                             280


  sequencing data. PhyClone is based on a Bayesian non-parametric model, the FS-CRP, which we develop in this work. A key                                    281


  feature of using the FS-CRP in PhyClone, is the ability to marginalise the clonal prevalence given the constraints imposed by                              282


  the tree. This allows us to perform collapsed inference in a parameter space that does not depend on the number of samples. As                             283
  bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.08.14.607069; this version posted August 17, 2024. The copyright holder for this
   preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in
                             perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license.

we demonstrate in the results, this improves the efficiency of sampling and accuracy of results as additional samples are used.           284


In addition, we have developed a Particle Gibbs sampler for efficient inference. A key innovation of this inference scheme is an          285


auxiliary variable construction over the sequence of points visited during each sequential Monte Carlo round.                             286


   The key limitation of PhyClone is the underlying PyClone model for correcting for mutational genotype. In particular, the              287


model is not capable of modelling regions of sub-clonal copy number variation. However, as the number of samples increases we             288


expect this deficiency to be less acute. For example, the HGSOC dataset analysed has numerous of sub-clonal copy number events            289


(McPherson et al., 2016, 2017). In future, the PyClone model could also be improved to adjust fo sub-clonal copy number variation         290


following approaches such as those in Frankell et al. (2023).                                                                             291


   PhyClone presents a robust and scalable Bayesian method for clonal history reconstruction from multi-sample bulk-sequencing            292


cancer data. Regardless of the source of synthetic data, PhyClone performed as well or frequently better than other state-of-the-art      293


methods. Furthermore, PhyClone was able to reconstruct accurate phylogenies in real datasets in the presence of mutation loss.            294


With its ability to leverage pre-clustering information, PhyClone easily scales to whole genome scale datasets. PhyClone also scales      295


well in the number of samples analysed in terms of running time and accuracy, allowing for extensive multi-region sequencing              296


datasets to be analysed.                                                                                                                  297
